Pier AI

A voice assistant for my school with a face: an animated mountaineer portrait of the patron, Pier Giorgio Frassati. You talk to it in Slovak with no button, and it answers out loud and in a speech bubble. It runs on a small model I pruned and fine-tuned myself, it knows my class's curriculum, and no recordings or transcripts are stored.

Overview

Pier AI is the voice of CSOŠE, the Frassati church technical secondary school in Bratislava. Students talk to it in Slovak. It knows facts about the school, tells the time and the bell schedule, reads out the timetable, gives teacher contacts and the weather, explains topics from the curriculum and tells jokes. It deliberately refuses to answer what it has no verified answer for.

The animation below follows one question through the whole system. Switch to “What time is it?” to see the shortcut: some things Pier answers exactly, without asking the AI at all.

The goal and the constraint

The decision I made early was that everything must run on a Raspberry Pi 5 (8 GB) with no PC, so a school could put it on a wall without a gaming machine or a cloud bill. That one constraint shaped every other choice: a small model, CPU speech recognition, and no dependence on paid services.

Today the full version runs on my home PC (RTX 3060) with a male voice. The Raspberry Pi version installs from a private GitHub repository with one command, but I have not yet run it on a real Pi, and I say so on purpose. What I have verified is the scripts, a simulation of the Pi on a PC without a GPU, and a remote voice.

What happens when you ask a question

  1. Listen. The microphone is always on and the app detects speech, so no button is needed. A student can interrupt Pier mid-answer with the on-screen button or the Space key.
  2. Transcribe. Parakeet-sk (int8, via sherpa-onnx) turns Slovak speech into text on the CPU. Audio is never written to disk.
  3. Fix names. fix_names corrects speech-model mistakes such as "farsati" to Frassati.
  4. Route. Python answers first whatever it can answer exactly: follow-ups like "repeat that" and "thank you" (the last two exchanges stay in memory for five minutes, never on disk), the time and date, teacher contacts from a curated list, unknown and unclear words, strict mode, and the timetable and bells.
  5. Retrieve. Otherwise up to three of the closest verified answers are found and added to the prompt. The search combines letter similarity with meaning (see below), so a question asked in different words still finds its answer.
  6. Generate. The local language model answers in a sentence or two at temperature 0.3, streaming sentence by sentence.
  7. Clean up. spellfix.fix restores missing diacritics and common small-model mistakes, only when the correction is unambiguous.
  8. Speak. Each sentence goes to the voice service with POST /speak, which returns a WAV that sounddevice plays.
  9. Show. The screen is a web page at http://localhost:8765; the app streams state changes to it over server-sent events, and the face moves its mouth, eyes and head to match.

Inside the brain

The language model does not look facts up and does not think like a person. It reads the question together with the retrieved verified answers (its cheat sheet) and computes which word should come next. It picks a likely one, adds it, and repeats until the sentence is done. That is why the cheat sheet matters so much: the model rephrases what it is given instead of recalling it.

Next-word prediction, one word per round. The percentages are illustrative; the real model has about two billion weights and chooses from about 60,000 word pieces.

Finding the right verified answer

A 2-billion-parameter model cannot memorise a whole curriculum, so the facts live in a collection of verified answers instead of in the weights. When a question arrives, knowledge.py scores every entry in two ways and adds them up:

  • Letters. Overlapping groups of four letters and word beginnings, weighted so rare words count more. It handles Slovak endings and small speech-recognition errors well.
  • Meaning. multilingual-e5-small, a 113 MB sentence-embedding model in quantised ONNX that runs on the CPU, turns the question into a vector and compares it with the pre-computed vectors of about 6,800 phrasings of the stored questions. It finds answers asked in completely different words.
  • Thresholds. An answer goes into the prompt above a combined score of 0.45, and lets a question through strict mode above 0.55. Without the meaning model the letter-only thresholds are 0.35 and 0.40.
  • Measured. On 32 new phrasings that are in neither the collection nor its variants, letters alone found 24, meaning alone 26, and the combination 27.

Routing and strict mode

Strict mode (PIER_STRICT=1, on by default) is the most important safety decision. The model may answer freely only on topics that were verified: the school, the patron, the weather, jokes, greetings, facts about Pier himself, the rules and places in the building. Anything else gets a fixed answer saying there is no verified answer.

  • Why. A small model outside its collection makes things up. Examples from testing: it turned "neštyri" into "the sign for the number three", and a garbled word into confused text.
  • The bypass. A question that matches a verified answer strongly enough (see the thresholds above) is answered even if its topic is not on the list, so adding a verified answer is the way to widen what Pier can do.
  • An ordering trap. Strict mode runs before the timetable. So the words students use for timetable and bell questions (hours, breaks, bells, timetable) must be on the allowed list, or those questions get refused before the timetable logic sees them.
  • When it is wrong. Strict mode also refuses simple general questions outside the collection. The remedy is to add verified answers, not to loosen the model.
  • Switching it off. PIER_STRICT=0, for experiments only.

The curriculum of my class

On 8 November I exported the full teaching plan of my class, III.GDMPS, from the school's EduPage: 12 subjects and 591 topics, each with the date it was taught. A script (ucivo.py) turns the plan into about 117 verified answers, such as what a subject covers, what we did last time, what comes next and what is inside a chapter, together with the ways students ask about it. Another 203 hand-written answers explain the topics themselves: networking and routing, maths, C++, PC hardware, Slovak inter-war literature, English and physics.

The plan is a snapshot. When the teachers move on, I export the pages again and rebuild the collection; the model itself does not need to change. Only my own class is visible to my account, so other classes would need their own access.

Making a small model speak good Slovak

An off-the-shelf small model speaks poor Slovak and invents facts. I trained my own.

Fine-tuning with LoRA: examples go in, small patches are learned on top of the frozen model, and the error falls. The curve shape is illustrative.

  • Pruning. The original Gemma 4 E2B has 5.1 billion parameters, of which about 2.75 billion are word tables for every language and 0.45 billion are audio and vision. I removed the audio and vision towers and cut the vocabulary from 262,144 to about 60,000 tokens. The model shrank to 2.5 billion parameters and the quantised file is 1.55 GB instead of 4.6 GB.
  • Fine-tuning. LoRA training on the pruned model: Slovak answer style, length, refusals ("I don't know, ask at the office"), the persona, reading verified answers from the prompt, and short dialogues. It trains only on the answer, on an RTX 3060 at about 590 to 650 tokens per second. Each new round replays part of the earlier data so nothing is forgotten.
  • Facts are not in the weights. The model learned to read facts from the prompt, including examples where the line is missing and the correct answer is "I don't know". When a fact changes, someone edits a text file and no retraining is needed.
  • Own prompt template. The Modelfile has a custom template identical to the one used in training. It must not be overwritten, and its FROM line is rewritten by the Pi installer.
  • No "thinking". For Gemma 4 models Ollama writes the answer into a thinking field by default, so the app sends reasoning_effort: none and the evaluation script needs --think-off.

A lesson from round 5

I trained twice overnight on the new curriculum answers, with a rule set in advance: only replace the running model if the new one is clearly better on the same 220 questions. Both attempts scored 202 against 204. They gained a question or two on the curriculum but lost on trap and general questions, so round 4 stayed. The curriculum already worked through retrieval (41 of 44). Facts belong in the collection; retraining is for changing how the model reads, not what it knows.

A lesson about teacher models

I first used a 12B Slovak model (OpenEuroLLM-Slovak) as a teacher to write general answers, and it invented facts (asked what the word "var" means, it answered "a Slavic calendar"). So every general answer is now written by hand in the answers_*.py files, and the teacher is only used to paraphrase questions.

A lesson about GPU memory

On a 12 GB card, Windows spills GPU memory into normal memory when it runs out, and training becomes about 3 times slower. Two fixes: round each batch length up to a multiple of 128 so memory does not fragment, and trim training samples longer than about 1,400 tokens (in round 5, by dropping the last retrieved answers from the longest prompts).

The face

The first version used a real photo with a WebGL effect. I replaced it with an illustrated SVG character because the patron is famous as a mountaineer ("Verso l'alto", towards the top) and a cartoon climber fits a school app better than an uncanny photo.

  • A round medallion with a halo, a pipe and an ice axe, drawn entirely in SVG (web/avatar.js, about 290 lines).
  • Head, eyes and mouth are driven by the app's state and the voice level while it speaks. The hand lifts the pipe away while it thinks and speaks.
  • The sky and the sun position follow the real time in Bratislava. A ?time=HH:MM parameter previews any hour.
  • The screen shows a clock card, a status pill, the speech bubble near the mouth, and an interrupt button.
  • Only public-domain artwork is used.

Things the model never guesses

Clocks, timetables and contacts are facts, so Python computes them and the model is never asked. The time and date come from the machine clock; bell times and the class timetable come from the school's EduPage data (timetable.py); teacher contacts come from a curated list (staff.py); weather comes from Open-Meteo for Bratislava, with no key. When speech recognition garbles a class name, the app does not guess a time; it asks the student to repeat it.

The spell-fix pass is deliberately cautious. It only restores missing diacritics, i versus y, and the Czech o that small models write instead of a, and only when exactly one candidate fits.

From my PC to the school's Pi

The project lives in three places: my home PC, where it is built and trained; a private GitHub repository, which keeps every version; and the Raspberry Pi at school, where it runs. Git works like save points in a game: each commit is a saved state of every file, a push sends it to GitHub, and a pull brings it to the Pi. Press the buttons to watch a first install and an update.

Large files cannot go into the repository (GitHub's limit is 100 MB per file), so the models travel as assets of a GitHub release.

On the Pi the whole install is four commands. The installer sets up Python, Ollama, the voice and a menu entry, and downloads the 2.1 GB of models from the release:

sudo apt install -y git gh
gh auth login
gh repo clone tomaskooooo/pier-ai ~/pier-ai
bash ~/pier-ai/pi/install.sh

After a change at home, git pull on the Pi is enough. After a new training round, pi/vydaj-modely.ps1 packs the model files on the PC for a new release. An offline USB bundle (2.4 GB) is still available for a school network that blocks downloads.

Keeping it up to date

  • New facts about the school go into data/skola.txt, which is read into every prompt. The model learned to read facts even when they change, so a new line works immediately.
  • New verified answers go into the train/answers_*.py files, and build_knowledge.py rebuilds the collection and its meaning vectors. No retraining is needed.
  • The curriculum is refreshed by exporting the EduPage pages again and rebuilding.
  • Every change is committed and pushed, and the Pi picks it up with git pull.

Privacy and licensing decisions

  • No transcript and no audio is ever written to disk. Logging recognised questions would need the school's agreement first.
  • The repository is private, because it contains school data and staff contacts.
  • The assistant refuses to reveal its own rules.
  • Licences are tracked honestly: Piper "lili" is MIT, Gemma 4 is Apache-2.0, and the OmniVoice weights are CC-BY-NC, so the male voice needs an assessment from the school's data-protection officer before it is used there.

Risks on the Raspberry Pi

The target is a Raspberry Pi 5 with 8 GB, active cooling and the original 27 W supply, a microSD card of at least 32 GB (NVMe is better), a USB microphone or headset, a speaker and a monitor, running Raspberry Pi OS 64-bit. Because it has never run on real hardware, these come first:

  • Whether sherpa-onnx, piper-tts and onnxruntime install cleanly for the Pi's Python version.
  • Whether Ollama on ARM recognises the Gemma 4 architecture.
  • The real speed, temperature (vcgencmd get_throttled) and memory (free -h, about 4 GB expected).
  • The microphone: the speech threshold is tuned to a PC headset.
  • Whether the school network allows GitHub and Hugging Face downloads (otherwise the USB bundle or a hotspot).

If it turns out too slow

  • Shorten the prompt and the answers, or lower num_ctx in the Modelfile.
  • Put only the relevant school facts into the prompt, which needs a short new fine-tune on the PC.

Known issues

  • Speech recognition errors can turn into a refusal or into "I did not understand the whole question".
  • Strict mode also refuses simple general questions outside the verified set, and a few general questions (Hamlet, the largest animal) still go wrong.
  • Piper's female voice has softer word starts that the recogniser sometimes mishears, which is why the sentence check is off on the Pi.
  • Holidays and school breaks are not in the timetable logic. Weather is fixed to Bratislava.
  • Only my own class's curriculum is included.

Engineering pitfalls

  • A full disk emptied a file. While testing quantisations the disk filled up and a file was silently truncated. Temporary GGUF and merged copies are deleted right after each experiment.
  • A regular expression that never matched. One with doubled slashes never matched, and an alternation joined with || was always true. Every new regex is tested on positive and negative examples.
  • Wrong test keywords. In the new curriculum tests, the model sometimes answered correctly with a different word form and failed the check. The keywords were fixed and the baseline re-measured, so the comparison stayed fair.
  • Two Python environments. The dataset scripts need the meaning-search model (onnxruntime), which only the system Python has; training needs the GPU environment.
  • Line endings. Shell scripts must have LF endings; the repository enforces it with .gitattributes.

Roadmap, in priority order

  1. Install it on the Pi from GitHub, measure the speed and fix whatever breaks.
  2. Shorten the prompt or answers if it is slow.
  3. Keep adding verified answers, based on what students actually ask.
  4. Refresh the curriculum during the year, and add other classes if the school gives access.
  5. Get the OmniVoice licence assessed if the male voice is to be used at school.

What I learned

  • A small model plus retrieval plus strict rules beats a bigger model with no guard rails for a school helper.
  • Put facts where they can be edited (text files and a retrieval collection), and put style where it must be learned (the weights). Round 5 proved it: retraining on facts did not beat retrieval.
  • Never replace a working model without a fair test on the same questions, and a rule decided before the result is known.
  • Rules beat a clever model where the answer is checkable: clocks, timetables, contacts and spelling.
  • Be honest about what has and has not been tested. The handover notes list the untested Raspberry Pi risks first.
