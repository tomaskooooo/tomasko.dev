Pier AI

A voice assistant for my school with a face: an animated mountaineer portrait of the patron, Pier Giorgio Frassati. You talk to it in Slovak with no button, and it answers out loud and in a speech bubble. It runs on local models, and no recordings or transcripts are stored.

Overview

Pier AI is the voice of CSOŠE, the Frassati church technical secondary school in Bratislava. Students talk to it in Slovak. It knows facts about the school, tells the time and the bell schedule, reads out the timetable, gives the weather, answers questions from the curriculum and tells jokes. It deliberately refuses to answer what it has no verified answer for.

The mascot is the school's patron, Pier Giorgio Frassati, drawn as the mountaineer he was. The sky behind him follows the real Bratislava sun, so the scene changes through the day. On Windows the app is a double-click icon; it opens a window, F or a double-click makes it full screen, and it shuts itself down when the window closes.

The goal and the constraint

The decision I made early was that everything must run on a Raspberry Pi 5 (8 GB) with no PC, so a school could put it on a wall without a gaming machine or a cloud bill. That one constraint shaped every other choice: a small model, CPU speech recognition, and no dependence on paid services.

Today the full version runs on my home PC (RTX 3060) with a male voice. A Raspberry Pi package is prepared, but I have not yet run it on a real Pi, and I say so on purpose. What I have verified is the syntax of the scripts, a simulation of the Pi on a PC without a GPU, and a remote voice.

What happens when you ask a question

  1. Listen. The microphone is always on. No button is needed; the app detects speech. The detection threshold (SPEECH_RMS) is tuned for a headset on the PC.
  2. Transcribe. Parakeet-sk (int8, via sherpa-onnx) turns Slovak speech into text on the CPU. Audio is never written to disk.
  3. Fix names. fix_names corrects speech-model mistakes such as "farsati" to Frassati.
  4. Route. In turn() the question goes through these checks, in this order: clock_answer (time and date), unknown_word_answer ("what does the word X mean" when X is not Slovak), unclear_answer (one word that is not a greeting), strict_answer (strict mode), and timetable.answer (timetable and bells, computed by Python).
  5. Retrieve. Otherwise the two closest verified answers from data/znalosti.jsonl are found by text similarity (minimum 0.40) and added to the prompt.
  6. Generate. The local language model answers in a sentence or two at temperature 0.3, streaming sentence by sentence (stream_llm).
  7. Clean up. spellfix.fix restores missing diacritics and common small-model mistakes, only when the correction is unambiguous.
  8. Speak. Each sentence goes to the voice service with POST /speak, which returns a WAV that sounddevice plays.
  9. Show. The screen is a web page at http://localhost:8765; the app streams state changes to it over server-sent events, and the face moves its mouth, eyes and head to match.

Routing and strict mode

Strict mode (PIER_STRICT=1, on by default) is the most important safety decision. The model may answer freely only on topics that were verified: the school, the patron, the weather, jokes, greetings, facts about Pier himself, the rules and places in the building. This is a regular expression, ALLOWED_TOPICS. Anything else gets a fixed answer saying there is no verified answer.

  • Why. A small model outside its collection makes things up. Examples from testing: it turned "neštyri" into "the sign for the number three", and a garbled word into confused text.
  • The bypass. A question that matches a verified answer with similarity of at least 0.40 is answered even if its topic is not on the list, so adding a verified answer is the way to widen what Pier can do.
  • An ordering trap. Strict mode runs before the timetable. So the words students use for timetable and bell questions (hours, breaks, bells, timetable) must be on the allowed list, or those questions get refused before the timetable logic sees them.
  • When it is wrong. Strict mode also refuses simple general questions outside the collection ("how many days are in a week?"). The remedy is to add verified answers, not to loosen the model.
  • Switching it off. PIER_STRICT=0, for experiments only.

On Windows the app starts OmniVoice by itself. If PIER_TTS_URL is set, no local voice is started and the remote one is used instead, which is how a Pi can borrow the PC's male voice over the private network. The sample rate is also a quick way to tell the two apart: I once benchmarked OmniVoice by mistake, thinking it was Piper, because port 8008 was already taken.

Making a small model speak good Slovak

An off-the-shelf small model speaks poor Slovak and invents facts. I trained my own.

  • Pruning. The original Gemma 4 E2B has 5.1 billion parameters, of which about 2.75 billion are word tables for every language and 0.45 billion are audio and vision. I removed the audio and vision towers and cut the vocabulary from 262,144 to about 60,000 tokens. The model shrank to 2.5 billion parameters, the quantised file is 1.56 GB instead of 4.6 GB, and picking a word is about 15% less work.
  • Fine-tuning. LoRA training on the pruned model with about 1,500 samples: Slovak answer style, length, refusals ("I don't know, ask at the office"), the persona, weather, rules and general curriculum answers. It trains only on the answer. It took about 80 minutes on the RTX 3060 at about 650 words per second.
  • Facts are not in the weights. The model learned to read school facts from the prompt, including examples where the line is missing and the correct answer is "I don't know". When a fact changes, someone edits a text file and no retraining is needed. On changed data it scored 5 out of 5.
  • Retrieval of verified answers. A 2-billion-parameter model cannot absorb a curriculum from a few hundred answers. So at question time the closest verified answers go into the prompt and the model rephrases them. On 20 brand-new topics it had never seen, it answered 19 correctly.
  • Own prompt template. The Modelfile has a custom template identical to the one used in training. It must not be overwritten, and its FROM line contains a Windows path that the Pi installer rewrites.
  • No "thinking". For Gemma 4 models Ollama writes the answer into a thinking field by default, so the app sends reasoning_effort: none and the evaluation script needs --think-off.
A lesson about teacher models

I first used a 12B Slovak model (OpenEuroLLM-Slovak) as a teacher to write general answers, and it invented facts (asked what the word "var" means, it answered "a Slavic calendar"). So every general answer is now written by hand in the answers_*.py files, and the teacher is only used to paraphrase questions.

A lesson about GPU memory

When batch lengths differ, GPU memory fragments, and on a 12 GB card Windows starts spilling it into normal memory, which is about 3 times slower. Rounding each batch length up to a multiple of 128 in train_lora.py fixed it.

The face

The first version used a real photo with a WebGL effect. I replaced it with an illustrated SVG character because the patron is famous as a mountaineer ("Verso l'alto", towards the top) and a cartoon climber fits a school app better than an uncanny photo. The photo version is kept in a backup folder.

  • A round medallion with a halo, a pipe and an ice axe, drawn entirely in SVG (web/avatar.js, about 290 lines).
  • Head, eyes and mouth are driven by the app's state and the voice level while it speaks (web/app.js). The hand lifts the pipe away while it thinks and speaks.
  • The sky and the sun position follow the real time in Bratislava. A ?time=HH:MM parameter previews any hour.
  • The screen shows a clock card at the top left, a status pill at the top and the speech bubble near the mouth.
  • Only public-domain artwork is used. Paintings with a watermark were rejected.

Things the model never guesses

Clocks and timetables are facts, so Python computes them and the model is never asked. The time and date come from the machine clock; bell times and the class timetable come from the school's EduPage data (a 370-line module, timetable.py); weather comes from Open-Meteo for Bratislava, with no key. When speech recognition garbles a class name, the app does not guess a time; it answers with a fixed request to repeat it.

The spell-fix pass is deliberately cautious. It only restores missing diacritics, i versus y, and the Czech o that small models write instead of a, and only when exactly one candidate fits. That cut unknown words from 2.5% to 2.2% without damaging any names.

Keeping it up to date

The school does not need me or a graphics card to improve it.

  • New facts about the school go into data/skola.txt, which is read from the prompt. The model learned to read facts even when they change, so a new line works immediately.
  • New verified answers are one line each in data/znalosti.jsonl in the form below. Write 1 to 2 sentences in standard Slovak with no invented facts. They work immediately without retraining.
{"question": "...", "answer": "..."}

One trap: at home I keep the same answers in train/answers_*.py and a build script generates znalosti.jsonl from them, overwriting it. Additions made directly in the school file therefore have to be copied into the answer files, or the next build erases them.

Privacy and licensing decisions

  • No transcript and no audio is ever written to disk. The launcher discards output and the voice services print nothing. Logging recognised questions would need the school's agreement first.
  • The assistant refuses to reveal its own rules.
  • The portrait is public-domain only.
  • Licences are tracked honestly: Piper "lili" is MIT, Gemma 4 is Apache-2.0, and the OmniVoice weights are CC-BY-NC, so the male voice needs an assessment from the school's data-protection officer before it is used there.

The Raspberry Pi plan

The target is a Raspberry Pi 5 with 8 GB, active cooling and the original 27 W supply, a microSD card of at least 32 GB (NVMe is better), a USB microphone or headset, a speaker and a monitor, running Raspberry Pi OS 64-bit (Bookworm or newer). The installer downloads packages, Ollama and the voice, so it needs internet; weather and timetable use it permanently.

  1. On Windows, a script builds a 2.2 GB bundle with the program, the language model, the recogniser and the voice service.
  2. Copy it to the Pi by USB stick or scp, then run the installer. It sets up the Python environment, Ollama, the voice, the pier-e2b model and a menu icon.
  3. The launcher starts Piper by itself and opens the screen in full-screen Chromium. Closing the window with Alt+F4 also stops the program.
  4. For a wall display, the autostart script plus automatic login starts it at power-on. I can administer the Pi from a school PC over SSH with keys.

Risks to check first, because it has never run on real hardware

  • Whether sherpa-onnx and piper-tts have ARM packages for the Pi's Python version.
  • Whether Ollama on ARM is new enough to recognise the Gemma 4 architecture (the PC runs 0.40.6).
  • The real speed, temperature (vcgencmd get_throttled) and memory (free -h, about 4 GB expected).
  • The microphone: the speech threshold is tuned to a PC headset, and devices must be selected with the variables above.

If it turns out too slow

  • Shorten the prompt and the answers.
  • Lower num_ctx in the Modelfile.
  • Put only the relevant school facts into the prompt, which needs a short new fine-tune on the PC.
  • A male voice on the Pi is not available yet, because there is no good Slovak male voice that runs without a GPU.

Known issues

  • Speech recognition errors can turn into a refusal or into "I did not understand the whole question". I saw this with synthetic questions and it is not always repeatable.
  • Strict mode also refuses simple general questions outside the verified set.
  • Piper's female voice has softer word starts that the recogniser sometimes mishears ("Veľká" heard as "dvetka"), which is why the sentence check is off on the Pi.
  • A single word that is not a greeting gets "say the whole question", and an unknown word in "what does X mean" gets "I do not know it".
  • Holidays and school breaks are not in the timetable logic. Weather is fixed to Bratislava.
  • Training and the large models exist only on my home PC; at school only the app and the data can be changed.

Engineering pitfalls

  • A full disk emptied a file. While testing quantisations the disk filled up and a file was silently truncated. Temporary GGUF and Ollama copies are deleted immediately after each experiment.
  • A regular expression that never matched. One with doubled slashes never matched, and an alternation joined with || was always true. Every new regex is tested on positive and negative examples.
  • Port 8008 is taken by OmniVoice while the app runs, so Piper has to be tried on another port, for example 8009.
  • Windows only. creationflags for subprocess.Popen exists only on Windows (fixed).
  • Line endings. Shell scripts must have LF endings, which the bundle script enforces. Editing files through scripts also needs care with CRLF and with \U in Windows paths inside Python strings.

Roadmap, in priority order

  1. Run it on the Pi, measure the speed and fix whatever breaks.
  2. Shorten the prompt or answers if it is slow.
  3. Add about 200 more verified answers, based on what students actually ask.
  4. Relax strict mode where it refuses without need, using real questions, without writing recognised questions to disk unless the school agrees.
  5. Get the OmniVoice licence assessed, with the school's data-protection officer, if the male voice is to be used at school.
  6. Optionally: a short new fine-tune that selects only the relevant facts for the prompt, and a good male voice for the Pi.

What I learned

  • A small model plus retrieval plus strict rules beats a bigger model with no guard rails for a school helper.
  • Put facts where they can be edited (text files and a retrieval collection), and put style where it must be learned (the weights).
  • Rules beat a clever model where the answer is checkable: clocks, timetables, weather and spelling.
  • Test every regular expression on both positive and negative examples.
  • Disk space and GPU memory are real resources when quantising and training models.
  • Be honest about what has and has not been tested. The handover notes list the untested Raspberry Pi risks first.
